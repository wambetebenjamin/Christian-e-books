Introduction 00

The Shift
That Changes Everything

You are not short of opportunities. You are short of visibility.

One of the biggest mindset shifts I ever had was this: clients aren’t hiding. They are not locked behind a secret door, a paid community, or a connection you do not have. They are listed, named, photographed, reviewed, and publicly contactable — and most people still say there is no work.

Open Google Maps. Search “restaurant” in your city. Scroll. Every pin is a business with a brand, a menu, a social account, a website that may or may not exist, staff schedules, customer messages, and a budget. Now search “dental clinic.” Then “gym.” Then “law firm,” “school,” “hotel,” “salon,” “car dealership,” “real estate agency.”

In one evening you can collect more qualified prospects than most people contact in a year. That is not a trick. That is simply looking.

“

The opportunity has always been there. The only new thing is that now you know where to look.

Three lies that keep people broke

“The market is saturated.” The market is saturated with people announcing that they are available. It is starving for people who show up with a specific idea for a specific business. Saturation is a problem of sameness, not of numbers.

“I need more experience first.” Experience is the thing you get after you ask. Nobody is going to award you readiness. You take it by doing the work for someone — sometimes for a small fee, sometimes for a case study, always for the proof.

“Nobody replies to cold messages.” Nobody replies to bad cold messages. A message that is clearly copy-pasted, all about you, three paragraphs long, and ends in “let me know” deserves the silence it gets. Chapter 4 fixes that permanently.

1 eveningis enough to build a list of 50 real, contactable businesses
10 / daypitches sent is the single habit that changes outcomes
3–8%reply rate is normal for a good cold pitch — so volume matters

How to use this manual

Read it once end to end. Then work it. Each chapter ends with a Do this now block — a small action you can finish in under an hour. If you do nothing but the action blocks, in thirty days you will have a lead list, an offer, a portfolio piece, a pitch you trust, and a pipeline of conversations.

Keep a single document open while you read. Call it Pipeline. Everything you build in this book lives there.

Do this now

Pick your three

Write down three services you can honestly deliver within a week of being hired — for example: social media content, a one-page website, product photography edits, short-form video editing, flyer and brand design, email writing, data cleanup, customer support, bookkeeping, virtual assistance. Three. No more. These become your offer in Chapter 3.

Every pin is a business.
Every business has a problem you can solve.

Chapter 01

The Map
Where Clients Sit

Nine hunting grounds, ranked by how fast they pay.

Finding clients is a sourcing problem before it is a selling problem. If your list is weak, your pitch cannot save you. Here is where to look, starting with the ground almost nobody works properly.

01

Google Maps

The richest, least-worked source on earth. Search a business category plus a location and you get names, phone numbers, websites, photos, opening hours, and reviews — the exact ingredients of a personalised pitch.

What to look for: no website at all, a website that breaks on mobile, fewer than ten photos, last review replied to never, a Facebook page used as the official site, blurry logo, no menu or price list.

02

Instagram & TikTok business accounts

Search a location tag or a hashtag like #nairobirestaurant or #lagosfashion. Businesses that post badly but post often are the best clients alive: they already believe in content, they simply need someone better.

What to look for: inconsistent feed, no captions or captions in all caps, good product but bad photos, reels under 300 views, no link in bio, DMs left unread in the comments.

03

Local directories and listings

Yellow-page style directories, chamber-of-commerce lists, trade association member pages, university supplier lists, event sponsor pages, and exhibitor lists from expos. An exhibitor list is a pre-qualified list of businesses that just spent money on marketing.

04

Job boards — read backwards

A job post is a confession. “We are hiring a social media manager” means we have a content problem and a budget. Pitch the hiring manager a freelance or trial version of the same outcome. Also apply — but pitching converts faster than applying.

Where: LinkedIn Jobs, Indeed, Wellfound, RemoteOK, WeWorkRemotely, Remotive, Upwork job feed (even if you do not bid), company career pages, Telegram and WhatsApp job groups.

05

LinkedIn, used as a search engine

Do not scroll the feed. Search job titles: founder, marketing manager, head of operations, filtered by location and industry. Follow, comment usefully for a few days, then message. Warm beats cold by a mile.

06

Communities where owners complain

Facebook groups for salon owners, Reddit subs for small business, Slack and Discord communities, WhatsApp groups for traders and SACCOs, Twitter/X replies under business accounts. Problems are stated out loud in public. Answer them helpfully, then offer to do it for them.

07

People already paying someone

Who sponsors podcasts? Who runs ads you keep seeing? Who has a “Website by —” credit in the footer? Who hired an intern last year? A business that has paid for help before will pay again without needing to be educated first.

08

Your own two-hundred

Your contact list, your church, your campus, your former classmates, your family’s friends, your old employer, the shop you buy lunch from. Most first clients come from here and most people skip it out of embarrassment. Send twenty “here is what I now do” messages before you send a single cold one.

09

Marketplaces as a floor, not a home

Upwork, Fiverr, Contra, Freelancer and PeoplePerHour are crowded and price-pressured, but they are a legitimate way to get your first three reviews and screenshots. Use them to manufacture proof, then migrate to direct outreach where the money is.

Rule of thumb

Spend 20% of your time sourcing and 80% reaching out. A perfect list that you never contact is a very organised form of procrastination.

The Google Maps walkthrough

  1. Choose a niche, not “everyone.” Pick one industry you can speak about: gyms, dental clinics, schools, hotels, law firms, bakeries, real estate. One industry means one pitch that improves with every send.
  2. Search “[industry] in [city or neighbourhood].” Zoom into one district at a time so results stay dense and local.
  3. Open each listing and record five fields: business name, owner or manager name if shown, phone, email or contact form, and the gap you noticed.
  4. Judge the website in ten seconds on your phone. Does it load? Is it readable? Is there a way to contact or buy? Are the photos decent? That is your opening line already written.
  5. Read the reviews. Complaints are a brief: “no one answered the phone” is an operations job; “couldn’t find the prices” is a website job; “didn’t know they moved” is a content job.
  6. Find the human. Check the website’s About page, the Facebook page’s admin, the Instagram bio, or LinkedIn for the owner’s name. “Hi Grace” outperforms “Dear Sir/Madam” every time.

Do this now

Twenty pins

Open Google Maps, pick one industry and one district, and log twenty businesses with the five fields above. Time yourself. Most people finish in under forty minutes and are genuinely shocked at how many gaps they find.

Chapter 02

The List
Fifty in an Evening

A lead you cannot contact is a daydream with a name.

A lead list is the only business asset you can build tonight for free. It compounds: the names you collect this week are still worth money next quarter, because businesses that ignore you in March get a budget in June.

The eleven columns

Build it in a spreadsheet. Nothing fancier survives contact with reality.

ColumnWhy it exists
Business nameObvious, but spell it exactly right in the pitch.
IndustryLets you reuse one pitch across similar businesses.
Contact personNamed humans reply. Info@ addresses rarely do.
ChannelEmail, Instagram DM, WhatsApp, LinkedIn, phone, walk-in.
Contact detailThe actual address or handle, ready to paste.
The gapOne specific observation. This is the heart of the pitch.
OfferWhich of your three services fits them.
Date contactedDiscipline lives in dates.
Follow-up 1 / 2Most replies come from follow-ups, not first sends.
StatusNew → Sent → Replied → Call booked → Won / Not now.
NotesEverything personal they tell you. Use it later.

Score before you send

Not every business deserves your evening. Score each lead out of five — one point for each yes — and pitch the fours and fives first.

+1They are clearly making money (busy location, many reviews, active sales)
+1There is a visible, nameable gap you can fix
+1You can reach a decision-maker directly, not a reception desk
+1They already spend on marketing, staff, or tools
+1You can show a relevant example or do a quick free sample

Leads to skip

Do not waste your best hours here

  1. Dead listings.No reviews in two years, disconnected number, abandoned page. There is no one to pay you.
  2. Giant corporates with no entry point.Procurement portals and “careers@” black holes. Go there later, with referrals.
  3. Businesses that only want free.If the first message is “do one for exposure,” believe them.
  4. Anyone you cannot actually serve.Do not pitch a 50-page e-commerce build if you have built one landing page.

Keep it legal and keep it human

Use business contact details that are published for business purposes. Identify yourself honestly, make it easy to say no, and stop contacting anyone who asks you to. One well-aimed message is worth a hundred blasts, and it keeps your email domain and your name clean.

Do this now

Fifty rows

Create the spreadsheet, add the eleven columns, and fill fifty rows across two or three industries. Score them. Sort by score. Tomorrow you are not looking for anyone — you are only sending.

Chapter 03

The Offer
Sell a Result

Nobody buys “graphic design.” They buy what design does for them.

Most beginners describe themselves with a job title and wonder why nobody responds. A title makes the buyer do the work of imagining how you help. An offer does that work for them.

✕

What beginners say

“Hi, I am a graphic designer and social media manager available for work. I am passionate, hardworking and a fast learner.”

Zero information the buyer can act on.
✓

What gets hired

“I turn restaurant menus into twelve ready-to-post photos and reels a month, so your Instagram stays active without the owner doing it at 11pm.”

A named outcome, for a named kind of business.

The offer formula

I HELP[a specific type of business]→GET[a specific result]→BY[the thing you actually do]

Examples you can adapt tonight:

  • I help dental clinics fill their weekday appointment slots by running their Instagram and WhatsApp follow-ups.
  • I help small hotels stop losing bookings by rebuilding their site into one fast mobile page with a booking button.
  • I help coaches turn one long video into ten short clips a week so they post daily without filming more.
  • I help busy founders get their inbox, calendar and invoices under control twelve hours a week.
  • I help shops that sell on WhatsApp get a clean product catalogue with prices, photos and descriptions.

Package it in three tiers

A single price invites negotiation. Three options invite a choice. Keep the names boring and the deliverables concrete.

Starter

The door opener

Small, fast, low risk. One landing page, one brand kit, eight posts, one edited video, a one-week audit.

Priced so a yes is easy.

Core

The real job

The full version of the result: the monthly content retainer, the five-page site, the complete rebrand, the weekly ops support.

This is what you want them to buy.

Plus

The upgrade

Core plus strategy, reporting, ads management, extra channels, or faster turnaround. It exists to make Core look sensible.

Some clients always take the top.

Pricing without flinching

Price on the value of the outcome and the time it costs you, never on how new you feel. Three rules keep you safe:

  1. Quote a project price, not an hourly rate, until you know exactly how long your work takes. Hourly punishes you for getting faster.
  2. Take 50% upfront. Not as a test of trust — as the normal way professional work is bought. No deposit, no start.
  3. Raise your price every three clients. If nobody has flinched at your price yet, it is too low.

And write down your floor: the number below which you will say no. Having a floor is what stops desperation from making your decisions.

Do this now

One sentence, three prices

Write your offer with the formula above, then set Starter, Core and Plus prices in your own currency. Say the Core price out loud five times until it stops sounding strange. You will say it on a call this month.

Reply

Short. Specific. About them.
That is the entire secret.

Chapter 04

The Cold Pitch
That Gets Replies

Five lines, one idea, one easy yes.

A cold pitch is not a cover letter, a CV, or an introduction to your life story. It is a single, specific, useful idea delivered to a busy person who has twelve seconds and a half-charged phone.

The anatomy

01

Proof you looked

One true, specific line about their business. Not “I love your brand.” Rather: “I saw your reels on the Saturday brunch menu are getting far more saves than the rest of your feed.”

02

The gap, stated kindly

Name one problem without insulting them. “Your site takes about nine seconds to load on mobile” is a fact. “Your site is terrible” is an argument you will lose.

03

The outcome

What changes if they fix it. More bookings, fewer calls, faster replies, cleaner brand, time back. Speak in their money and their hours.

04

The proof

One link, one screenshot, one sentence. A sample you already made for them beats any portfolio you could send.

05

A tiny ask

Not “let me know if interested.” Ask a closed question: “Want me to send the three-shot sample I mocked up?” or “Is Thursday 3pm okay for ten minutes?”

“

If your pitch could be sent to a hundred businesses without changing a word, it will be ignored by all hundred.

Three pitches you can send tonight

Email — the gap pitch

Subject: Quick note about the booking page on [business] ’s site

Hi [Name],

I was looking at [Business] after a friend mentioned your [specific thing]. On my phone, the booking page takes about nine seconds to load and the button sits below the fold — which is usually where most mobile enquiries drop off.

I rebuild pages like this into one fast mobile page with the booking button at the top. For [similar business], that took enquiries from the site from 4 to 11 a week.

I already sketched what yours could look like. Want me to send the mockup? No charge either way.

— [Your name], [one-line credential or portfolio link]

Instagram DM — the sample pitch

Hi [Name] — your [product] photos are genuinely good, but the reels stop at about 200 views, which usually means the first second isn’t hooking.

I edited one of your existing clips into a 12-second version with a hook and captions. Took me 20 minutes. Want me to send it over? If you like it, I do 8 of these a month.

LinkedIn — the job-post reversal

Hi [Name], I saw [Company] is hiring a social media manager. While that role is open, I can cover the posting and community replies on a short freelance basis — two weeks, fixed fee, no commitment after.

Here are three posts I wrote in your voice from your last campaign: [link]. If it helps, I can start Monday. If you’d rather I just apply formally, I’ll do that instead.

The follow-up is the job

Most people send once and conclude that cold outreach does not work. A reasonable sequence is four touches over two weeks, each one adding something new rather than nagging.

Day 0The pitch
Day 3“Here is the sample I mentioned” — attach it, no pressure
Day 7Share a relevant idea or a competitor example
Day 14The polite close: “Should I close your file for now? Happy to check back in a few months.”

That last message gets more replies than any other, because it gives people permission to say no — and many of them would rather say yes.

Instant delete triggers

Remove these from every message

  1. “I hope this email finds you well.”Six wasted words at the exact moment attention is highest.
  2. Your life story.Your course, your passion, your availability. They care about their business.
  3. “Dear Sir/Madam” and “To whom it may concern.”Proof you did not look. Find the name.
  4. Five paragraphs.If it does not fit in a phone screen, it will not be read.
  5. “Let me know if interested.”Ends with homework for them. Ask one closed question instead.
  6. Attachments on first contact.Use a link. Attachments land in spam and nobody downloads from strangers.

Do this now

Ten sends

Take the top ten leads from your list and send ten personalised pitches. Give yourself five minutes per message, including the personal line. Log the date. Then close the laptop — refreshing the inbox is not a work activity.

Chapter 05

Proof
With Zero Clients

You do not need permission to do the work. Only to be paid for it.

The chicken-and-egg problem — no clients because no portfolio, no portfolio because no clients — is only a trap if you believe a portfolio must contain paid work. It must contain work. Nobody checks the invoice.

Four ways to manufacture proof this week

01

The unsolicited redesign

Pick a real local business. Rebuild their homepage, reshoot their menu, redesign their logo, or re-edit their reel. Publish it as a before-and-after with your reasoning. It doubles as a portfolio piece and a pitch.

02

The invented brand

Create a fictional business in your target niche and build its full identity: logo, colours, five posts, a landing page, a brand voice note. It proves range even though no client existed.

03

The three free ones

Offer three businesses a free, small, time-boxed piece of work in exchange for a testimonial and permission to publish it. Say “free” once, with an end date. Never run an unpaid retainer.

04

The public build

Post what you are learning and making every week — teardowns, process threads, before-and-afters. Over time the feed becomes a portfolio that works while you sleep and brings inbound leads.

What a case study must contain

Three paragraphs and two images beats twenty pretty mockups:

  1. The problem. What was broken and what it was costing them.
  2. What you did. Decisions, not just deliverables. Why this layout, this hook, this colour, this process.
  3. The result. Numbers if you have them; honest observations if you do not. “Load time went from 9s to 1.4s” is a result. So is “the owner now posts in 10 minutes instead of an hour.”

Where to put it

One link. Not six. A simple Notion page, a one-page personal site, a Behance or Dribbble profile, a Google Drive folder with a clean cover — any of these is fine as long as it opens fast on a phone and shows your best three pieces first. Put the link in every bio you own.

Do this now

One piece, published

Choose a business from your lead list and make one real thing for them — a redesigned page, three edited photos, a 15-second reel, a rewritten About section. Publish it with a short write-up. Then send it to them. That single asset is portfolio and pitch in one.

Chapter 06

Remote Jobs
& Internships

The front door is crowded. Use the side entrance.

Four hundred people apply through the form. Four message the hiring manager with a relevant sample. You already know which pile gets read. Treat a job application as a pitch with extra paperwork, and your odds change completely.

The stack that actually works

01

Apply properly, once

Submit the form with a CV tailored to the posting’s own words. Applicant tracking systems match language, so mirror their phrasing for tools and responsibilities — honestly.

02

Find the human

Search the company on LinkedIn for the hiring manager, team lead, or founder. For small companies it is almost always the founder. Guess the email format if needed: first@, first.last@, f.last@.

03

Send the sample, not the plea

“I applied for the content role. Before you read my CV, here are three posts I wrote for your product and the reasoning behind them.” Do the job before you get the job.

04

Make yourself findable

Headline that states the outcome you deliver, a banner, a pinned portfolio link, and weekly posts about your craft. Recruiters search by keyword; be the keyword.

05

Ask for the internship directly

Many internships are never advertised. A short note — “I would like to intern with your team for three months, remotely, starting [date]; here is what I would work on in week one” — creates roles that did not exist.

The remote-ready checklist

01A professional email address with your real name
02A one-page CV in PDF, named Firstname-Lastname-Role.pdf
03A portfolio link that loads on mobile in under three seconds
04A usable headshot and a clean, quiet corner for video calls
05Reliable internet, a backup connection, and a stated timezone
06Fluency in the basics: Google Workspace, Slack, Zoom, Notion or Trello
07A calendar link so nobody has to negotiate times with you
08Replies within 24 hours, always. Responsiveness is a skill they are testing

Interviews: three answers to prepare

“Tell me about yourself.” Ninety seconds: what you do, who you do it for, one proof point, why this role. Not your childhood.

“You lack experience in X.” Agree without apologising, then bridge: “Correct, I have not managed paid ads at scale. I have run three small campaigns at a total spend of X with these results, and here is how I would approach your first month.”

“What are your salary expectations?” Give a researched range with a reason, and ask for theirs first when you can: “For this scope, I am looking at X–Y. What range has the role been budgeted at?”

Protect yourself

Remote-work red flags

  1. They ask you to pay for training, equipment, or a “registration fee.”Employers pay you. Always.
  2. Hired after a two-message chat on WhatsApp or Telegram.Real companies interview.
  3. An unpaid “test task” that is a full deliverable.A test is two hours. A campaign is a job. Charge for it.
  4. Cheques to deposit and forward.That is money laundering, and you carry the liability.
  5. No contract, no scope, no company footprint.If you cannot find the business anywhere, neither can a court.

Do this now

Five applications, five messages

Apply to five remote roles today. For each one, find a human and send a short message with one piece of relevant work attached as a link. That is five applications and five pitches in the same hour.

Chapter 07

The Call
& the Close

They replied. Now do not talk yourself out of the job.

A discovery call is not a performance. It is a diagnosis. The person who asks the better questions controls the conversation, and the person who controls the conversation sets the price.

The twenty-minute structure

  1. Two minutes — frame it. “Thanks for the time. I want to understand what you are trying to fix, then tell you honestly whether I am the right person. Sound good?”
  2. Ten minutes — ask and shut up. What are you trying to achieve this quarter? What have you tried? What happens if nothing changes? Who else is involved in deciding? When do you want it done?
  3. Five minutes — play it back. “So the real issue is not the logo, it is that customers cannot tell what you sell within five seconds.” When you name their problem better than they did, price stops being the main topic.
  4. Three minutes — propose and book. Outline the approach, give a range, and agree the next step with a date. Never end a call without a next step in a calendar.
Say this

“Based on what you have described, this usually lands between X and Y. I will send a short proposal today with a fixed price and a timeline.”

The one-page proposal

Long proposals lose deals. One page, sent within 24 hours, containing exactly six things:

01Their goal, in their own words
02What you will deliver, listed as items
03What you will not do, so scope stays honest
04Timeline with dates and what you need from them
05Price, deposit, and payment schedule
06One sentence: “Reply ‘approved’ and I will send the invoice and start Monday.”

The four objections, answered

“It is too expensive.”

“Understood. I can do a smaller first phase at [Starter price] that covers the most urgent part, and we expand if it works.” Reduce scope, not price.

“Send me your rates and I will get back to you.”

“Happy to. So the numbers are useful, can I ask two quick questions about what you need?”

“We will do it in-house.”

“Makes sense. If it helps, I can set the system up and train whoever runs it, so it is a one-off cost rather than ongoing.”

“You are new at this.”

“True — which is why I work in small paid phases and you can stop after the first. Here is what I delivered for [example].”

Protect the work

Every job, however small, needs four things in writing: the scope, the price, the number of revisions, and the payment terms. A two-paragraph email that both of you reply to is a contract. Take 50% upfront, deliver on time, invoice the same day you finish, and follow up on late payment without apology — politely, repeatedly, in writing.

Then ask for two things on delivery day, while they are happy: a one-line testimonial and one referral. That single habit converts a first client into a pipeline.

Do this now

Build the template

Write your one-page proposal template today, before you need it. When a reply comes in, you should be twenty minutes from sending a proposal, not two days.

Chapter 08

The 30-Day
Sprint

Motivation is unreliable. A schedule is not.

Two focused hours a day for thirty days. Week one builds the machine. Weeks two to four run it. Do not redesign your logo on day nineteen.

Week 1

Build the machine

  • Choose one niche and three services
  • Write your offer sentence and three prices
  • Build the 50-row lead list and score it
  • Create one portfolio piece and publish it
  • Write your pitch, follow-ups and proposal templates

Week 2

Go loud

  • 10 pitches a day, five days — 50 total
  • Send follow-up 1 to everyone from day three
  • Apply to 10 remote roles, message 10 humans
  • Post twice about your work
  • Review: which opening line got replies?

Week 3

Convert

  • 10 pitches a day with your improved line
  • Run every call with the 20-minute structure
  • Send proposals within 24 hours, every time
  • Deliver any small paid job early
  • Ask every happy contact for one referral

Week 4

Compound

  • Close the loop with the day-14 polite close
  • Turn delivered work into two case studies
  • Add 50 new leads for next month
  • Raise your price if you closed two or more
  • Keep the ten-a-day habit. Forever.

The daily two hours

What the block looks like

  1. 30m
    Source

    Add ten new leads with a named gap for each.

  2. 60m
    Send

    Ten personalised pitches and all scheduled follow-ups.

  3. 30m
    Make

    Build one small thing — a sample, a post, a case study, a template.

The only four numbers to track

SentPitches out the door this week
RepliesAny human response, yes or no
CallsConversations booked
ClosedPaid work or offers accepted

If replies are low, your opening line is wrong. If replies are fine but calls are low, your ask is too big. If calls are fine but closes are low, your proposal or price needs work. The numbers tell you which chapter to re-read.

The last page

Nobody is coming
to discover you.

There is no list you are missing, no secret group, no gatekeeper quietly deciding your turn. There is a map you have not opened, a list you have not built, and ten messages you have not sent today.

The businesses are there. The budgets are there. The remote roles are posted right now. The only variable left is whether you look — and whether you keep looking on the days nobody replies.

The ten-a-day rule

Ten messages a day. Four follow-ups per lead. One new piece of proof a week. Do that for ninety days and you will not recognise your calendar, your inbox, or your bank balance. The opportunity has always been there. Now you know where to look.
